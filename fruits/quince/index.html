🟡 Quinces

Cydonia oblonga

How to grow quinces at home: hardiness zones 5–9. Quinces are self-fertile.

Growing · Rootstocks · Grafting · Propagating · 122 varieties

How to grow quinces

Sun

Full sun: at least 6–8 hours of direct sun a day. Tolerates light shade.

Soil

Most soils, including heavier and moist ones, pH 6.0–7.0.

Spacing

12–15 ft (3.5–4.5 m).

Planting

Plant while dormant.

Care

Low maintenance. Water in drought. Fruit stays hard; it is cooked, not eaten raw.

Pruning

Prune lightly in winter; keep an open, bushy shape.

Harvest

Mid to late autumn when golden and very fragrant; pick before hard frost.

First crop

3–5 years.

Common problems

Fire blight and quince leaf blight.

Through the year

Spring

  • Enjoy the large pink-white flowers.

Summer

  • Water in dry spells.

Autumn

  • Pick golden fruit; store apart from apples.

Winter

  • Light pruning.

Grafting quinces

Sometimes grafted Quinces root easily, so many grow on their own roots. Named varieties are also grafted onto quince rootstocks.

What it joins with

Quince rootstocks (Quince A, Quince C, BA29) and quince seedlings. Quince can also be grafted onto pear or hawthorn.

When to graft

Late winter to early spring; summer for budding.

Scion wood

Cut pencil-thick one-year-old shoots in mid to late winter, while dormant. Keep them damp and cold in the fridge until you graft.

Best methods

  1. Whip and tongue: Onto quince rootstocks.
  2. Chip bud: In summer.

Propagating quinces

Quince roots far more easily than apple or pear. Quince rootstocks are themselves made by mound layering.

From seed

No, and seedlings are slow.

Ways to make more plants

  1. Hardwood cuttings: Take thick 8–10 in (20–25 cm) cuttings; use rooting hormone.
  2. Mound (stool) layering: The standard way to make many plants at once.
  3. Chip bud: To put a named variety on quince rootstock.
