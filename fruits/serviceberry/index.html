🫐 Serviceberries

Amelanchier alnifolia and spp.

How to grow serviceberries at home: hardiness zones 2–8. Serviceberries are self-fertile.

Growing · Rootstocks · Grafting · Propagating · 25 varieties

How to grow serviceberries

Sun

Full sun to part shade.

Soil

Most well-drained soils, pH 6.0–7.5.

Spacing

6–10 ft (2–3 m); can be grown as a hedge.

Planting

Plant in spring or autumn.

Care

Very hardy. Net against birds when fruit ripens.

Pruning

Remove the oldest stems every few years to keep the bush young.

Harvest

Early summer, when berries turn deep purple.

First crop

2–4 years.

Common problems

Birds, cedar rust and saskatoon sawfly.

Through the year

Spring

  • Enjoy the white flowers.

Summer

  • Net and harvest.

Autumn

  • Enjoy the autumn color.

Winter

  • Thin old stems.

Grafting serviceberries

Sometimes grafted Most serviceberries grow on their own roots, but named varieties can be grafted, often as small standard trees.

What it joins with

Serviceberry seedlings, hawthorn, mountain ash (Sorbus) and sometimes pear.

When to graft

Late winter to early spring; summer for budding.

Scion wood

Cut pencil-thick one-year-old shoots in mid to late winter, while dormant. Keep them damp and cold in the fridge until you graft.

Best methods

  1. Whip and tongue: Onto serviceberry seedlings.
  2. Chip bud: In summer.
  3. Cleft: Onto a hawthorn or mountain ash trunk for a small standard tree.

Propagating serviceberries

Many serviceberries spread by suckers, the easiest way to get more. Named varieties are also grown from softwood cuttings.

From seed

Close, but not exact. Seed needs 90–120 days cold.

Ways to make more plants

  1. Suckers and division: Dig rooted shoots from the edge of the clump.
  2. Softwood cuttings: Take them early, just as new shoots firm up; keep very humid.
  3. Seed: Give the seed 90–120 days cold.
