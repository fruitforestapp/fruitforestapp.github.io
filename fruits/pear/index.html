🍐 Pears

Pyrus communis

How to grow pears at home: hardiness zones 4–9. Most European pears need a second pear variety that blooms at the same time; a few (Kieffer, Seckel) set some fruit alone.

How to grow pears

Sun

Full sun: at least 6–8 hours of direct sun a day.

Soil

Tolerates heavier soil than apples, pH 6.0–7.0, but must not stay waterlogged.

Spacing

Quince or OHxF dwarfing stocks 10–12 ft (3–4 m); standard trees 18–25 ft (5.5–8 m).

Planting

Plant while dormant in early spring, graft union above the soil.

Care

Feed lightly: too much nitrogen makes soft growth that fire blight attacks.

Pruning

Prune in late winter; train to a central leader with wide-angled branches. Cut out fire blight 8–12 in (20–30 cm) below damage.

Harvest

Pick European pears mature but still hard, when they lift off with a twist, then ripen them indoors for 1–2 weeks.

First crop

3–6 years.

Common problems

Fire blight, pear psylla and pear scab. Pears ripen badly on the tree (brown, gritty centers).

Through the year

Spring

  • Watch blossoms for fire blight in warm wet weather.

Summer

  • Thin to one or two fruit per cluster.

Autumn

  • Pick hard and ripen indoors.

Winter

  • Prune and remove blighted wood.

Grafting pears

Usually grafted Pears are grafted onto pear rootstocks for full-size or semi-dwarf trees, or onto quince for small trees.

What it joins with

Pear seedlings and rootstocks (OHxF, Pyrodwarf), quince, and sometimes hawthorn or serviceberry. Some varieties, Bartlett (Williams) among them, are incompatible with quince and need a short piece of a compatible pear such as Old Home or Beurré Hardy in between (an interstem).

When to graft

Late winter to early spring for bench grafts; spring for top-working; mid to late summer for budding.

Scion wood

Cut pencil-thick one-year-old shoots in mid to late winter, while dormant. Keep them damp and cold in the fridge until you graft.

Best methods

  1. Whip and tongue: Onto pear or quince rootstocks; pears take very readily.
  2. Cleft: To top-work an older tree to a better variety.
  3. Chip bud: In summer.
  4. Bark (rind): For thick limbs.

Tip: Pears are among the easiest fruits to graft, so they are a good place to practise.

Propagating pears

Pears are grafted onto pear seedlings, pear rootstocks such as OHxF, or quince for smaller trees.

From seed

No.

Ways to make more plants

  1. Whip and tongue: Onto pear or quince rootstocks in late winter. Some varieties, Bartlett (Williams) among them, don't join well with quince directly and need a short piece of a compatible pear (an interstem) between them.
  2. Chip bud: In summer.
  3. Cleft: To top-work an old tree in spring.
